# Writing patterns

Use this page as an editing guide, not as a forbidden-word dictionary. Context determines whether a phrase is doing useful work.

## Delete the announcement

Weak: “In today's fast-paced world, having visibility into your assets is more important than ever.”

Better: “See which assets have missing owners.”

Weak: “Here are some key considerations to keep in mind before proceeding.”

Better: “Check the scope and owner before you start.”

## Prefer a real consequence

Weak: “This change will significantly improve the user experience.”

Better: “The new filter lets analysts show unassigned findings without searching every row.”

Weak: “There are several important challenges associated with the process.”

Better: “The PM has not defined the empty state, and engineering needs an answer before implementation.”

## Remove automatic persuasion

Weak: “It's not just about speed. It's about unlocking a more intuitive, seamless future.”

Better: “The new flow removes two confirmation steps.”

Weak: “The future starts with one decision.”

Better: “Choose the workspace to continue.”

## Don't overcorrect

- Keep a qualification when it prevents a false claim: “may,” “usually,” and “approximately” can matter.
- Keep passive voice if the actor is unknown: “The payment was declined” is useful UI copy.
- Keep short sentences when urgency or comprehension calls for them.
- Keep a three-item list if there are exactly three real items.
- Don't force “you” into technical documentation where the direct subject is clearer.

## English and Hebrew

- Prefer plain language in either language.
- Preserve natural Hebrew syntax rather than translating English copy patterns literally.
- For English text inside Hebrew interfaces, use ordinary casing unless a real acronym or brand requires capitals.
- Avoid decorative English section kickers in otherwise Hebrew layouts.
